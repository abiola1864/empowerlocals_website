Programme · September 4, 2026

Fifty-nine traders,
five weeks, one
WhatsApp group.

What we learned running the LSETF MSME bootcamp in Agege, from the numbers the entrepreneurs left behind.

← Back to Blog

By ELP Foundation · September 2026

Every Wednesday morning through April, a room in Agege filled up with people who run small businesses. A tailor. A caterer. A woman who sells pure water. A man who runs a haulage service. Fifty-nine of them in all, there to spend five weeks learning how to run their businesses a little better, together with the Lagos State Employment Trust Fund and our team at ELP.

In between the Wednesday sessions, the classroom moved to WhatsApp. Our quiz assistant, EmpowerBot, sent questions to everyone's phone, marked their answers on the spot, and kept a running leaderboard the whole group could see. That meant something we do not always get in this kind of work: a record of who was learning, how much, and where things slipped. When the programme ended with a graduation on 30 April, we sat down with that record. Here is what it told us.

59entrepreneurs took part
81%actively joined in
3,388quiz answers
74%were women

Who showed up, and who stayed

Getting people to register is easy. Getting them to keep going, week after week, while they are also running a business and a home, is the hard part. Most of our group cleared the first hurdle: 81 percent answered at least one quiz. But the further along the programme went, the smaller the group at the front of the class became. By the end, 18 people had answered every single question we sent.

We are not discouraged by that drop. If anything, it points straight at what to fix: a nudge, a check-in, a small reason to come back in week three. It is a solvable problem, and now we can see exactly where it happens.

The ones who kept coming back learned the most

This is the finding we keep returning to. When we lined up each person's quiz scores against how many quizzes they had taken, the pattern was hard to miss. The people who dipped in once or twice averaged 61 percent. The people who went all the way averaged 87. Showing up, it turns out, is most of the battle.

The lesson is not teach harder. It is help more people keep going.

Did it actually work?

Fair question, and the honest answer is: for the people who engaged, yes. Three out of every four active participants scored 70 percent or higher, the mark we treat as real competency. Among the group who finished everything, every single one cleared it. Across the whole cohort, people got 2,914 quiz answers right. That is a lot of business knowledge that was not there in March.

Who these entrepreneurs are

Nearly three-quarters of the room were women. That matters to us, and what matters more is that they did not just fill seats. Women took part at the same rate as men and scored a touch higher on average. Whatever else this programme did, it worked for the people it was mainly built for.

What they told us they need

When we asked about their biggest obstacle, the answers were almost unanimous. Three in four said the same thing: not enough cash. Not marketing, not competition, not skills. Money to buy stock, money to buy equipment, money to grow. Half named "funds to grow" as their single most pressing need.

The other quiet finding was about record-keeping. Of everyone who told us how they track their business, exactly one person used accounting software. The rest relied on notebooks, phone notes, or memory. You cannot plan for growth, or make the case to a lender, on numbers you keep in your head. That is a gap the next phase has to close.

Where we go from here

Three things follow from all this. First, the appetite for capital is real and specific, and small, well-targeted grants or loans, in the range of a few hundred thousand naira, would go a long way. Second, record-keeping needs to move from a single lesson to ongoing, hands-on support, because that is where the weakest businesses lose the most ground. Third, we want to hold onto more of our people through the middle of the programme, and now we know precisely where they drift.

One honest note for next time: this round measured how well people did during the programme, but not how much they gained from where they started, because we ran one continuous set of quizzes rather than a before-and-after test. A simple baseline quiz on day one would let us show, cleanly, exactly how far each person travelled. We will build that in.

Fifty-nine people gave us five weeks of their attention, and a great deal of data with it. The least we can do is listen to what it says. To the graduates of Agege: congratulations, and thank you for teaching us as much as we hoped to teach you.
